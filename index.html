    How this was made

    Method, data & sources

    This atlas grew out of Session 6 (“Choosing Ground”) of the course Design an Agrarian Industrial Commune at The Convent, Greenpoint, Brooklyn. It is decision-support for groups asking where in the world a commune actually pencils out — not legal advice. Confirm anything load-bearing with a local lawyer before you act.

    The triangle

    Every siting decision trades three things against each other: quality of life, the cost of land, and how hard it is for a whole group to immigrate. You rarely get all three. The strongest places hand you two and let you finesse the third — depopulating interiors with cheap land and an easy passive-income visa, or easy-residency countries with middling services, or high-QoL countries that will take you only if you bring money.

    The six lenses (0–3 each, composite 0–18)

    LensWhat scores a 3What scores a 0
    LANDRural land is cheap by global standards and foreigners (or a foreign-owned entity) can own it freehold; cheap-house or abandoned-village programmes existLand is expensive, or foreigners are barred from farmland and only long leases exist
    VISAA realistic route for every adult in a group: passive-income or rentista visa with a modest threshold, fast processing, short path to permanent residencyOnly work-sponsored or investor routes; long or uncertain path to residency
    TAXTerritorial system or a special regime that leaves foreign income, pensions and co-op surpluses lightly taxedWorldwide taxation at high rates, wealth tax, no special regime
    LAWPurpose-built communal vehicles (land co-ops, community land trusts, foundations) and zoning that lets a group build and farm togetherNo communal vehicle, hostile or opaque zoning, communal title unrecognised
    LIFESafe, healthy, good public healthcare that new residents can join, temperate climate, services reach the countrysideUnsafe, thin healthcare, extreme climate, services absent where land is cheap
    STABStable politics, strong rule of law, sound currency, peace, climate-resilientFragile state, weak courts, currency crises, conflict exposure

    Scores are opinionated and comparable, not precise. They were first set in a July-2026 synthesis for the course deck, then re-scored country by country in October 2026 by research agents instructed to move a score only when verified evidence warranted it; the rationale and the original score are shown on every dossier.

    Your weighted fit

    The six sliders weight the lenses 0–5. Fit = Σ(weight × score) ÷ Σ(weight × 3), shown as a percentage. The presets are starting points — “Cheap land + visa” (LAND 5, VISA 5, TAX 2, LAW 3, LIFE 1, STAB 1), “Low-tax remote” (TAX 5, VISA 4, LIFE 3), “Safe & high QoL” (LIFE 5, STAB 5, LAW 3), “Hedge” (STAB 5, LAND 3, LIFE 3), “Balanced” (all 3).

    Resilience model

    Stability is broken into six 0–100 dimensions — political, rule of law, safety, economic/currency, peace/conflict, climate — each grounded in a published index (Global Peace Index, WJP Rule of Law Index, Transparency CPI, Fragile States Index, ND-GAIN, Numbeo) with a one-line reason per dimension.     Where the numbers come from

    1. Jurisdiction dossiers. One research agent per country, working from primary sources (immigration and tax authorities, PwC/KPMG/EY tax summaries, Eurostat, USDA, FAO, national statistics, index publishers, immigration-law firms) and asked to list every realistic visa route, current farmland prices in USD/ha, foreign-ownership rules, tax treatment of foreign income and pensions, healthcare access for new residents, agricultural support, the actual legal vehicles a commune could use, climate and water risk, and 3–10 existing communities. Every claim carries a URL.
    2. Adversarial fact-check. A second, independent agent per country tried to refute the nine most load-bearing claims (ownership status, land price, visa thresholds, residency and citizenship timelines, tax system, cost, indices, healthcare access, existence of the listed communities). Verdicts and corrections are shown on each dossier under “Fact-check”.
    3. Communities. A Wikidata harvest of every item typed intentional community, ecovillage, kibbutz, cohousing, moshav or Hutterite colony that has coordinates; plus 29 regional and network sweeps (GEN, FIC, Eurotopia, Diggers & Dreamers, cohousing directories, Bruderhof, Twelve Tribes, Camphill, Hutterite colony lists, Anastasia settlements, Asian and African networks, historic utopias) each followed by a checker that flagged fakes, duplicates and bad coordinates; plus the communities surfaced by the country research. Every point was then tested against country polygons, and duplicates were merged by name and distance.

    Caveats

    • Visa thresholds, tax regimes and ownership rules change constantly (several did in 2025–26: Portugal’s citizenship clock, Spain’s golden visa, Italy’s crypto rate). The build date is shown at the bottom of the left panel.
    • Farmland prices are regional ranges in USD per hectare for the cheapest usable land, typical productive land, and prime land. They are indicative, not appraisals.
    • Communities data is a census of what is findable online. Coordinates for some entries are the village they sit in, not the gate. “Historic” means the community no longer exists in its original form.
    • Kibbutzim and moshavim in the occupied West Bank and Golan Heights are labelled by the territory Wikidata records them in; the atlas takes no position.
    • Agents can be wrong. Where the fact-checker and the researcher disagreed, both are shown.
